# Hooks

The hook is everything above "...see more". On a phone that's about two lines. Most people decide in that space whether to read on, so it gets more care than any other part of the post.

## The three-question check

Every hook should answer these for the reader before the cut:

1. **Who's it for?** The right reader recognises themselves. ("Founders selling to hospitals", "if you run a 20-person agency".) This is the one most often missing. A punchy hook that doesn't tell the reader it's for them still loses.
2. **What's it about?** The subject is clear by line two. No warm-up.
3. **What do I get?** A lesson, a number, a fix, a story with an ending. The reader knows the payoff is coming.

## Formatting rules

- Use two short lines with a blank line between them. Keep each under about 60 characters so neither wraps on a phone.
- Don't let a sentence spill over the cut.
- No bold, no fancy unicode, no emojis in the first two lines unless that's the user's style.
- Make them want line three. Don't give away the payoff in the hook.

## Hook types

Pick the one that fits the idea and the person. Rotate them so the feed doesn't read like a formula.

| Type | Template | Illustrative example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credential | I've [done the thing] for [time]. [Lesson you wouldn't expect]. | I've sold payroll software for nine years. The demo never closes the deal. |
| Shouldn't be true | [Good thing] almost [bad outcome]. | Our biggest customer almost put us out of business. |
| Exact number | [Specific result] in [time]. [What caused it]. | We cut onboarding from 21 days to 2. All we did was remove one step. |
| Before and after | [Low point]. Now [high point]. | Year one we had 3 customers. Now we turn away 3 a week. |
| Contrarian | [Common advice] is wrong for [audience]. | Most B2B founders hire their first salesperson too early. |
| Cliffhanger | [Scene, one line]. [Line that raises the stakes and stops]. | The customer asked for a call on a Friday at 5pm. I knew what was coming. |
| Proof of work | After [big exact number] of [effort], [finding]. | After 200 sales calls this year, one question predicts who buys. |
| List promise | [Number] [things] I [learned / ranked / wish I'd known]. | Every pricing model we've tried, ranked by what it did to churn. |
| Question | [Question the buyer can't answer quickly]. | How many hours did your team spend on payroll corrections last month? |

The examples are made up to show the shape. Real hooks use the user's real numbers and stories.

## When to use which

- **Credential** is strong for founders and senior operators with years in the field. Don't use it twice in a row, it starts to read as bragging.
- **Exact number** and **proof of work** only work with exact numbers. "Thousands of calls" kills it. Be exact or pick another type.
- **Cliffhanger** only works when the story pays off. A small ending after a big setup feels like a trick.
- **Contrarian** needs a real position the user holds and can defend, from `stories.md`. Never invent disagreement.
- **Question** works best when the buyer would feel a little uncomfortable answering it.

## Two-step method

1. **Say it straight.** Write what the post promises in plain words. It must pass the three-question check.
2. **Then make it sharper.** Apply one device: a specific number, an unexpected detail, a short understatement, a flip from a flaw to a win. One device only. Stacking them reads as trying too hard.

## Common mistakes

- Vague numbers ("we grew a lot"). Use the exact figure.
- Generic topics ("5 tips for growth"). Add who it's for and something only this founder knows.
- Jargon the buyer doesn't use. Use their words from the interview.
- Opening on the company name or a launch announcement.
- A hook that promises more than the post delivers.
